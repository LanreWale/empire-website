Hourly vs. Project Pricing

Both pricing models work â€” but they work differently. Here's when to use each.

Hourly

$X/hr
Best for ongoing work with unclear scope. Protects you when projects drag on.

Project

$X flat
Best for well-scoped work. Rewards you for efficiency â€” finish faster, earn more per hour.

Retainer

$X/mo
Best for ongoing relationships. Predictable income for you, priority access for the client.

When to charge hourly

  • The project scope is unclear or likely to expand
  • The client wants ongoing work with variable volume
  • You're starting a new client relationship and want to build trust first
  • The work involves maintenance, revisions, or open-ended tasks

When to charge per project

  • The scope is well-defined and unlikely to change
  • You're efficient at the task and can finish faster than average
  • The client prefers predictable costs (most do)
  • You've done similar work enough times to estimate accurately

When to offer a retainer

  • The client needs regular, ongoing work
  • You want predictable monthly income
  • You've built a strong working relationship with the client
  • The scope can be defined as "X hours per month" or "X deliverables per month"

Pro tip: Most experienced freelancers use a mix. Project pricing for well-scoped work, hourly for uncertain scope, and retainer for their best 2â€“3 clients. The proposal templates in this toolkit include language for all three models.

How To Raise Your Rates Without Losing Clients

The single biggest financial move most freelancers avoid is raising rates. Here's how to do it without losing the clients you want to keep.

Give Adequate Notice

Provide existing clients at least 30 days notice before rate increases. Frame it as an annual review of your pricing rather than a sudden change. Most professional clients expect periodic rate adjustments â€” especially if you've been delivering strong results.

Quantify Your Value

When announcing a rate increase, reference specific results you've delivered. "The landing pages I wrote last quarter generated a 34% increase in your conversions" is far more persuasive than "I need to raise my rates." Numbers make the conversation about ROI, not cost.

Offer A Transition Period

Consider honoring your current rate for 60â€“90 days on projects already in the pipeline. This shows goodwill and gives clients time to adjust their budgets.

Word-for-Word Negotiation Script

Opening the conversation:

"Hi [Client name], I wanted to give you advance notice about a rate adjustment. Effective [date, 30 days out], my rate will be [new rate]. This reflects both my continued investment in [skill/software/training] and the results we've been able to deliver together. I wanted to give you plenty of notice so we can plan the pipeline accordingly."

If they ask why:

"I've been evaluating my pricing across all clients, and I'm aligning my rates with the current market. I've also been investing in [specific skill or software], which has directly improved [outcome for them]. I'm confident the increased rate reflects the value I'm delivering."

If they push back on price:

"I understand budget is tight. I'm happy to keep our current rate for projects already on the calendar, or we can look at adjusting the scope to fit the new budget. What works best for you?"

If they threaten to leave:

"I completely understand if this doesn't fit your current budget â€” I'd rather you tell me than have an uncomfortable working relationship. If you'd like, I can help transition the work to someone else at a lower rate. And of course, my door is always open if you want to work together in the future."
