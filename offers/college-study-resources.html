Why Good Study Habits Matter More Than Intelligence

Research from the University of California found that students who use structured study methods score 20–30% higher on exams than students who rely on passive re-reading. The difference isn't talent — it's technique. Students who use active recall, spaced repetition, and structured note-taking consistently outperform their peers regardless of their natural aptitude.

The average college student studies 15–20 hours per week, but most of that time is spent inefficiently. Highlighting textbooks, re-reading notes, and cramming the night before an exam are among the least effective study methods according to cognitive science. Yet they remain the most popular because they feel productive — you're moving your eyes across text and it seems like learning.

The 7 Study Methods That Actually Work

1. Cornell Notes

Divide the page into notes, cues, and summary. Write notes during class, add cues after, and summarize at the end. The cues become your self-quiz questions.

2. Mind Mapping

Start with a central idea and branch outward to related concepts. Visual connections improve memory retention by up to 32% compared to linear notes.

3. Active Recall

Close your notes and write down everything you remember. Then check what you missed. This is the single most effective study technique identified by cognitive research.
